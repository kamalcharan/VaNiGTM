    The Idea Glut

    Why prioritization — not technology — is the executive problem in enterprise AI.

    A Monday in March

    The CEO of a mid-size pharmaceutical company returns from an industry conference with eleven AI ideas. By Friday, the CIO has added nine from vendor briefings, the head of commercial wants a field-force copilot she saw at a competitor, and a well-meaning data science team has built a demo that summarises clinical trial protocols — impressively, for the three documents it was tuned on.

    Nothing about this company is dysfunctional. Every idea is plausible. Several are excellent. And that is precisely the problem: the organisation now holds twenty-two candidate initiatives, one budget, and no shared way to choose. What happens next, in most companies, is that choosing quietly defaults to other mechanisms — seniority of the sponsor, recency of the conference, quality of the demo. The portfolio gets decided without ever being designed.

    Fig 1.1 — Twenty-two initiatives. No axes. Bubble size = seniority of the sponsor; colour = where the idea came from. Notice what's missing: any dimension that measures the ideas themselves. By Chapter 4 you'll have the axes; by Chapter 7, every bubble will have earned its place — or been removed.
    "The scarce resource isn't ideas. It's the judgment to rank them."
    The premise of this module

    Why now: the discipline gap

    AI capability has improved enormously in two years — call it 100× across model quality, cost per task and ease of integration. But be precise about what that number is: it is laboratory capability, not realized enterprise value. Between what the technology can do and what your organisation actually captures sit four gates — procurement, data, process and adoption — and each one taxes the multiple. Meanwhile, organisational discipline for choosing and governing AI work has improved perhaps 2×. The distance between those two curves is the discipline gap, and it is where AI initiatives fail.

    Fig 1.2 — Capability is not value. The 100× is what benchmarks measure. Realized value = capability × organisational discipline — and the four gates tax the multiple before anything reaches a P&L. Closing the gap is a leadership project, not a technology purchase.

    The visible symptoms of the gap are consistent across industries: a growing count of pilots and a static count of production systems; AI spend rising while no one can name the metric it moved; teams quietly running "shadow AI" because the official initiatives don't touch their real work; and a leadership team that alternates between enthusiasm and anxiety, quarter by quarter, because it has no instrument for telling signal from noise.

    ⚠ Trap — starting with the technology

    "We should do something with agents" is a technology looking for a task. Every durable AI initiative in this module starts from the opposite end: a task, with an owner, done today at a known cost, that a capability could do better. Technology-first framing is the single most reliable predictor of a stalled pilot.

    What prioritization actually buys you

    Focus — three funded use cases with owners beat twenty-two ideas with sponsors. Defensibility — when the board asks "why these three?", the answer is seven scored dimensions, not a persuasive meeting. Speed — paradoxically, ranking slows you down for one workshop and speeds you up for four quarters, because every downstream decision (budget, hiring, data work) inherits the ranking. Kill authority — a scored portfolio gives leaders licence to stop things publicly, which frees more organisational energy than any launch ever does.

    The seven dimensions behind those scores — three measuring impact, four measuring feasibility — are previewed below and fully anchored in Chapter 4:

    Impact Value poolStrategic fitMeasurability Feasibility Data readinessProcess maturityRisk & compliance loadChange load
    ✓ In practice

    The discipline transfers. One BFSI chief operating officer described the shift: before, AI proposals arrived as slide decks with a sponsor attached; after, nothing reached her desk without its seven scores and its 2×2 position. The meetings got shorter. The arguments got better. The portfolio got smaller — and delivery got faster.

    Key takeaways
  • Idea abundance, not idea scarcity, is the real state of enterprise AI — and it silently breaks unstructured decision-making.
  • The 100× is lab capability; realized value = capability × discipline, taxed by four gates: procurement, data, process, adoption.
  • Prioritization buys focus, defensibility, speed and the authority to kill — the four things demos never provide.

    What Counts as a Use Case

    A use case is a task, not a technology — and most prioritization failures begin as definition failures, three weeks before anyone scores anything.

    The granularity problem

    Ask a leadership team for AI ideas and you'll receive three species of answer. Ambitions: "become an AI-first company." Projects: "AI for the finance function." Tasks: "extract line items from vendor invoices into the ERP." Only the third can be scored, because only the third has edges — you can name who does it today, how often, what it costs, and what "done well" looks like. Ambitions and projects aren't wrong; they're simply upstream. Every ambition decomposes into projects, and every project into tasks. Scoring happens at task level. Always.

    Fig 2.1 — The decomposition ladder. Every ambition hides projects; every project hides tasks. Scoring only works at the right-hand end, where the four-part granularity test can be answered.

    The four-part granularity test

    TestQuestionIf you can't answer…
    OwnerWhich role performs this task today?It's a project wearing a task's clothes — decompose further.
    FrequencyHow often does it run — per day, week, case?You can't size the value pool, so impact scoring will be fiction.
    OutputWhat artifact or decision does it produce?You can't define "done", so you can't measure quality or ROI.
    Quality barWhat does "done well" mean, and who judges it?You can't set the human-review threshold — a governance gap in waiting.

    Two altitudes: the task view and the workflow view

    There's a second definitional axis, and it matters enormously in regulated industries. A use case can sit inside one step of an existing workflow (the task view), or it can redesign the workflow itself (the workflow view). Both are legitimate. But they are different animals: the task view leaves the process, roles and SOPs intact — AI assists inside a step, a human verifies, and the change footprint is small. The workflow view moves the steps themselves — and with them roles and responsibilities, SOPs, training records, and (in GxP contexts) validation scope. The rule: score at task level, plan change at workflow level. Workflow ambitions decompose into task use cases for scoring — but if several tasks in one flow score well, you are really holding a workflow redesign, and its change load must be planned as one.

    Pharma example 1 — Adverse-event case processing

    Task view · AI inside one step
    "Extract AE data points from intake emails into the safety database"
    Intake → extraction (AI drafts, human verifies) → triage → narrative → QC. The flow is untouched; step 2 gets faster. The case processor's role becomes review-first.
    Change footprint Roles · minimalSOPs · unchanged Training · briefValidation · extraction step only
    Workflow view · flow redesigned
    "Redesign AE case processing around AI: intake, triage, narrative and consistency checks"
    Four AI-assisted steps re-sequenced; seriousness triage reordered before data entry; case processors become case reviewers; QC sampling model changes.
    Change footprint Roles · redefinedSOP-PV series · revised Training · re-certificationValidation · full process

    Pharma example 2 — Health-authority query management

    Task view · AI inside one step
    "Draft responses to authority queries from the dossier and prior correspondence"
    Query received → drafting (AI, from dossier + precedent) → medical/regulatory review → approval → submission. Writers review instead of writing from blank pages.
    Change footprint Roles · same chainSOPs · unchanged Training · prompt & review skillsValidation · n/a (human-approved)
    Workflow view · flow redesigned
    "End-to-end query management: AI routing, drafting, review-chain orchestration and submission tracking"
    Queries auto-classified and routed; drafts generated with citations; review chain compressed from four gates to two; a new QC gate owns AI-output sampling.
    Change footprint Roles · writers → editorsSOP-RA series · revised Training · new review disciplineValidation · routing logic
    ◈ Risk — the hidden workflow use case

    The most expensive definitional mistake: funding what looks like a task use case that is quietly a workflow use case. If adoption requires people to work differently — new handoffs, new review duties, revised SOPs — the change load is workflow-sized regardless of how small the AI component is. Chapter 4's change-load dimension exists to catch exactly this.

    AI's native verbs

    Task-level thinking gets easier when you know what current AI is naturally good at. Most high-feasibility enterprise use cases are built from six verbs. If a candidate contains none of them, look harder at what the task actually is — or whether it's a task at all.

    Draft
    first versions: emails, narratives, code
    Extract
    structure from documents
    Summarise
    long input → decision-ready
    Classify
    route, triage, tag
    Match
    records, clauses vs standards
    Monitor
    watch streams, flag exceptions
    ⚠ Trap — the vague use case scores well on everything

    "AI for customer service" will score 4 on impact (customer service matters!) and 3-ish on feasibility (surely something is possible?) — and both numbers are meaningless. Vagueness inflates scores because the scorer fills gaps with optimism. If a use case scores well but you couldn't brief a team on Monday to build it, it failed the granularity test — sharpen before scoring.

    Key takeaways
  • Ambition → project → task: scoring only works at task level, where owner, frequency, output and quality bar are nameable.
  • Two altitudes: score at task level, plan change at workflow level — workflow use cases move roles, SOPs, training and validation, not just a step.
  • High-feasibility use cases are usually built from AI's native verbs: draft, extract, summarise, classify, match, monitor.
    • Reflection
  • Take the vaguest AI idea circulating in your organisation. Decompose it: what are its three most valuable task-level use cases?
  • Pick one candidate you consider a "task" use case. If it succeeded and spread, which SOPs and roles would actually have to change — and is it secretly a workflow use case?

    Sourcing: The Four Lenses

    A portfolio built from one discovery method is a biased portfolio. Run all four scans — plus process mining as the evidence-based fifth — before you score anything.

    Fig 3.1 — Five ways in, one gate out. The four lenses rely on what people say about work; process mining reads what systems recorded. Only the union is trustworthy — and nothing gets filtered until everything is scored.

    Running each lens

    A lens you can't run is a slide, not a method. Each block below is the operating card: what the lens finds, the steps, the time it takes, and what it hands to the inventory.

    Lens 1 · The industry scan
    ~half a day · desk research
  • Collect deployments, not announcements — peer companies and adjacent industries; for each candidate ask: what daily volume, what human review around it?
  • Translate the pattern, not the product — insurance claims-triage maps to pharmacovigilance intake; banking KYC extraction maps to clinical site onboarding.
  • Log each as a task-level candidate in your organisation's vocabulary.
    • Finds / misses

    Finds: proven patterns with reference economics.
    Misses: anything specific to your data or your bottlenecks.
    Pharma example: competitor's medical-information auto-drafting → your MedInfo response desk.

    Lens 2 · The role & function walk
    ~1 week · interviews
  • Walk the org chart — 30 minutes per function head, using the three-question hour audit (below).
  • Chase the queues, not the opinions — "what work waits for you?" beats "where could AI help?" every time.
  • Write candidates as tasks before leaving the room: owner, frequency, output, quality bar.
    • Finds / misses

    Finds: unglamorous compounders nobody pitches at conferences — which is why they're feasible.
    Misses: cross-functional flow problems no single function owns.
    Pharma example: QA reading batch records for known deviation patterns.

    ✓ The three-question hour audit — ask every function head

    1. Where do your skilled people spend repetitive, judgment-light hours?   2. What work queues up — and how long does the queue get?   3. What gets done badly under time pressure at month-end, quarter-end, or submission deadlines?
    At Meridian, the walk surfaced: medical affairs drafting standard-response letters (Q1), QC deviation classification queuing 11 days (Q2), and field-force CRM notes written from memory on Friday evenings (Q3). Three candidates, three different lenses' blind spots covered by one conversation.

    Lens 3 · The task-pattern scan
    ~2 hours · checklist
  • Take the six native verbs — draft, extract, summarise, classify, match, monitor.
  • Sweep each major operation: where does this verb happen at volume, on text or documents?
  • Mechanical by design — this lens catches work nobody thinks of as "an AI idea," merely as work.
    • Finds / misses

    Finds: high-frequency micro-tasks invisible to interviews.
    Misses: value context — pair its output with Lens 2's queue evidence.
    Pharma example: "match: purchase orders ↔ contract terms" in procurement — never pitched, high volume.

    Lens 4 · The data-asset scan
    ~half a day · with data owner
  • Invert the question: what data do we already hold that AI could finally put to work?
  • List the sleeping assets — years of field-call notes, every deviation report since 2015, all authority correspondence.
  • Apply the three usability tests (below) before any asset earns a candidate.
    • Finds / misses

    Finds: high-feasibility candidates — the hardest dependency already exists.
    Misses: use cases needing data you don't hold yet.
    Pharma example: 8 years of authority queries + final responses = a precedent library for RA drafting.

    ■ Data issue — held ≠ usable

    A data asset only counts if you can answer three questions: is it consolidated (or scattered across systems and inboxes)? Is it permissioned for this purpose (consent, contracts, regulation)? Is it trusted (would the process owner bet a decision on it)? Score the data you can use, not the data you have.

    The fifth method: process mining

    The four lenses share a weakness: they produce opinions about work. Process mining produces evidence of work — it reads the event logs your ERP, CRM, QMS, ticketing and workflow systems already record, and reconstructs how work actually flows: where cases queue, where handoffs loop, where exceptions eat the hours. That difference matters most at exactly the moment you're about to automate, because the two classic AI-automation disasters are both invisible to interviews.

    01
    Automating the imagined process
    Interviews describe the happy path. Logs reveal the process as it runs — variants, exception loops, workarounds. AI designed for the imagined process fails on contact with the real one, and that failure is normally discovered after funding. Mining moves the discovery before scoring.
    02
    Codifying SOP drift
    The pharma-specific disaster. If mined reality deviates from the documented SOP, both automation options are bad: automate the SOP version (it fails — that's not how work flows) or automate the actual version (you've hard-wired non-compliance into a system). Mining surfaces the drift while it's still a process question, not an audit finding.

    Why this reduces the risk gap

    The risk gap in AI automation is the distance between the process you scored and the process you'll actually deploy into. Mining closes it by converting three of the seven scoring dimensions from opinion into evidence:

    DimensionWithout mining — assertedWith mining — measured
    Process maturity"The process is fairly standard" (the owner's belief)Variant count and exception rate, from the logs
    Value poolEstimated volumes and cycle timesReal volumes, real queues, real cycle times
    MeasurabilityA baseline study someone must fund laterThe mined baseline is the before-picture — re-mine after deployment and ROI attribution is built in

    Meridian, mined: the AE case flow

    Before scoring its safety-operations use cases, Meridian pulled twelve months of event logs from the safety database — roughly 9,000 cases. Three findings changed the portfolio:

    Fig 3.2 — What the logs said that interviews didn't. The work is ~6 hours; the case takes ~11 days. The delay lives in queues, not effort — and 28% of cases loop back for rework. (Figures illustrative, within the fictional Meridian case.)

    The three findings, and what each did to the portfolio:

    1 · The rework loop is real. 28% of cases bouncing from QC back to data entry is evidence that extraction quality (UC #1) attacks an actual loop, not an assumed one — the use case's value-pool score gained a defensible rationale.

    2 · The money sits in wait-time. Six hours of work inside eleven elapsed days means drafting-type use cases attack the small number. A classify/triage use case — routing and prioritising cases the moment they arrive — attacks the queues, and re-ranked above two ideas the leadership team liked better. This is mining's signature move: it changes which use case to fund first.

    3 · One sub-process isn't ready. Expedited cases following 12 distinct paths is a variance flag: that flow needs standardisation before automation. Its feasibility score was honestly reset — parked, with a named pre-condition, instead of funded and failed.

    ◈ Risk — SOP drift, the GxP special

    Meridian's mined flow also showed intake teams routinely re-ordering two steps the SOP sequences differently — harmless in practice, invisible in interviews, and exactly the kind of drift that becomes an audit finding when an AI system codifies it. The rule: reconcile mined reality with the documented SOP before automating either. Where they disagree, fix the process or fix the document — never let the AI system inherit the disagreement.

    ✓ In practice — lightweight process mining

    You don't need a mining platform to start. Pull 90 days of timestamps from one system — ticket created / assigned / resolved, or case received / triaged / closed — and chart the queues between steps. One pharma operations team found that 60% of change-control cycle time sat in a single "awaiting QA classification" queue: a classify-verb use case no interview had surfaced, because everyone had normalised the wait.

    Impact × Feasibility

    Two questions, seven anchored dimensions, one honest portfolio. This chapter is the module's engine — everything before it prepares the inventory; everything after it uses the scores.

    Why anchored scales

    Unanchored scoring drifts within weeks: the CFO's "4" and the CIO's "4" describe different worlds, and the portfolio becomes a negotiation dressed as analysis. An anchor fixes what each end of the scale means in observable terms, so scores are portable across scorers and defensible under challenge.     Data Readiness Deep-Dive

    Most feasibility optimism dies on the data line. This chapter is where the program's entire data story lives — the five gates, the ownership map, the regulatory split, the delta checks — packaged as the Vikuna Data Readiness Audit.

    The reframe: readiness is use-case-relative

    Two reflexes kill more AI portfolios than any technology gap. The first: "our data isn't ready for AI" — paralysis. The second: "let's build the data platform first" — a two-year detour. Both make the same mistake: treating readiness as an enterprise-absolute property. It isn't. Readiness is use-case-relative: a use case scores 4 when its own data chain — the specific sources this task reads and writes — passes five gates. Meridian's safety mailbox can be ready while its supply-chain lake is a swamp. Score the chain, not the enterprise.

    Δ1
    Drift over time
    The data the AI was designed and evaluated on vs the data arriving at runtime.     Δ2
    Cross-system variance
    The same fact holding different values in different systems — a product code in LIMS vs ERP, a case status in the safety DB vs the tracker spreadsheet. An AI reading both inherits the disagreement. Check: for every fact the use case consumes, name the single source of truth — and what happens when the systems disagree.
    ⚠ Trap — evaluating on yesterday, deploying into tomorrow

    Pilots evaluate on historical, curated data — runtime serves live, drifting data. A 96% extraction accuracy on last year's cases is an upper bound, not a forecast. The delta checks exist so the gap between those two numbers is planned for, not discovered.

    ROI & Value Logic

    Where value claims either survive a CFO or don't. The unit of measurement, the honest bridge from gross to net — including the cost of critical thinking — and the guardrails that keep net value real after go-live.

    The unit of value: the process value chain

    Here is the module's third altitude rule, and it changes how every ROI claim is written. You score at task level (Ch 4). You plan change at workflow level (Ch 2). And you measure value at process-value-chain level — the end-to-end flow from trigger to outcome: case received → case submitted; query received → response filed; order placed → cash collected.

    Why not task level? Because task savings are hostages to the chain. Meridian's UC #1 saves 25 minutes of touch-time per case at the data-entry step — but the mined map (Fig 3.2) showed six hours of work inside eleven elapsed days. A task improvement that pours time into the next queue moves no metric a CFO can find. And why not function level? Because functions don't improve — chains do. "Finance has improved" is not a measurable claim; "cost per submitted case fell 18% and cycle time fell four days" is. The chain is where the baseline lives, where the mined evidence lives (Ch 3), and where attribution is honest.

    "You can no longer say finance improved. The value chain improved — and the chain has a number."
    The unit-of-value rule
    Fig 6.1 — Three altitudes, three disciplines. The chain metric is the only one a CFO can audit: cost per case, cycle time, throughput, right-first-time rate. Task metrics are leading indicators — useful, but never the ROI claim itself.

    The five value pools

    Every chain-level value claim draws from one of five pools. Name the pool before naming the number — CFOs discount each pool differently, and the hardest discounts fall on the pools claimed most casually:

    The value bridge: gross to net, honestly

    The bridge is the chapter's engine and the capstone's CFO-challenge exhibit. It starts from gross chain value and subtracts the three costs that enthusiasts forget — including the one almost nobody prices: the cost of critical thinking.

    Fig 6.2 — The value bridge. Gross ₹45.0L shrinks 41% on the way to net — and the biggest subtraction is the human one. A bridge that shows no critical-thinking cost is advertising unreviewed AI output in a pharmacovigilance chain — which no CFO, and no auditor, should accept.     The cost of critical thinking

    AI shifts humans from doing to verifying — and verification is real, sustained cognitive work, not a glance. Price it at the rate of the person qualified to catch the error, not the rate of the person who used to do the typing. And plan for its failure mode: vigilance decay. As drafts get good, review quietly degrades into rubber-stamping — which silently returns the quality risk the review existed to catch, while the cost line keeps being paid. The guardrails below exist largely for this.

    ◈ Risk — the handover is where quality lives

    The human handover point is a designed thing: what the reviewer sees (diff against source, not a finished-looking document), how much they review (100% at launch, sampled as evidence accumulates — with the sampling decision made by QA, not by the budget), and what they can do (correct, reject, escalate). A use case without a designed handover doesn't have a review — it has a signature.

    The error ledger: the confusion matrix, for executives

    The critical-thinking line pays for reviewing what the AI surfaces. But AI quality is not one number, and the errors that hurt most are the ones that never surface. Every AI decision splits four ways — and the two error types carry asymmetric business costs. Meridian's seriousness-triage use case makes it concrete:

    Fig 6.3 — The error ledger. One vocabulary sentence is enough: precision controls false positives, recall controls false negatives — and where FN cost is catastrophic, you tune toward recall and accept more review load. That threshold is a business decision about error appetite, not a data-science setting.

    Why false negatives mislead

    FN·1
    Invisible by construction
    False positives generate a human touch — someone reviews, rejects, complains. False negatives generate nothing: no ticket, no correction, no signal. Your dashboard fills with the errors you catch and stays clean of the ones you miss — the system looks best exactly where it's failing.
    FN·2
    Accuracy hides them
    If 2% of cases are serious, a model that flags nothing is 98% accurate — while missing 100% of what matters. "96% accurate" is a meaningless claim until you ask: accurate on which class? Demand the per-class split, always.
    FN·3
    Human review doesn't cover them
    The critical-thinking line pays for reviewing what the AI surfaced. In a triage or classify use case, what the AI didn't flag, no human ever reads. Review protects you from false positives; only deliberate blind sampling of the negative stream protects you from false negatives — which is why it's a guardrail below, not an optional extra.

    The bridge integration: expected error cost = (FP rate × FP cost) + (FN rate × FN cost), priced asymmetrically, entered against net value. For Meridian's triage: an FP costs six review minutes; an FN risks a late expedited report. No single "accuracy" number could have priced that.

    ⚠ Trap — the vendor number, and the Data DNA question

    "Trained on 40 million records. 96% accurate." Neither number tells you what you need. Training volume is not transferability, and blended accuracy is not safety. What's missing is exactly what this section taught: the confusion-matrix split on your critical class — and the Data DNA match: how closely does the data the system was trained and evaluated on resemble yours — case mix, document formats, terminology, languages, source systems, edge-case frequencies? A model evaluated on US call-centre English is a different product when it meets your Hinglish field-force notes and 14 attachment formats. Four questions to put in every vendor evaluation: (1) Show the confusion matrix on our critical class — not blended accuracy. (2) What is the false-negative rate at the threshold you recommend — and what happens to it when we tune toward recall? (3) What data was this evaluated on, and how does its DNA match ours? (4) Will you run against our gold-standard evaluation set before contract? — the 500-case set from Chapter 5's pre-conditions register just became your vendor lie-detector. A vendor who won't answer all four is quoting a demo, not a system.

    The four ways ROI math lies

    01
    Capacity released ≠ cost saved
    Freeing 3,750 hours saves nothing until those hours are redeployed to named work or removed from the cost base. Until then it's an option, not a saving — bridge it as capacity, priced only when the redeployment is real.
    02
    Pilot economics, extrapolated
    Cost-per-case at 50 curated cases is not cost-per-case at 9,000 live ones — context grows, retries multiply, edge cases arrive (Δ1, Ch 5). Re-price the bridge at scale volumes before anyone quotes it.
    03
    Attribution theft
    Three initiatives claiming the same cycle-time delta. Chain-level baselining is the fix: one chain, one baseline, one ledger of what changed and when. Mining (Ch 3) gives you the ledger for free.
    04
    Gross sold as net
    The commonest lie, and the bridge exists to kill it: no value number leaves the room without its run cost, critical-thinking cost and change cost subtracted — in writing.

    Measurement design: decided before build

    Three commitments, made when the use case is funded — not after go-live: a named chain metric (cost per submitted case, cycle time, right-first-time rate), a named owner (the chain's owner, not the AI team), and a baseline before build — which the Ch 3 mined map already gave you: the risk-gap thread completes here, as the same evidence that de-risked scoring becomes the before-picture that makes ROI attributable.

    The cost of failure: Project Athena

    Why so much discipline about waves, absorption and patience? Because the cost of a visible failure is not priced at the initiative level. A fictional story that every real organisation will recognise:

    ◈ The story — three years before the Monday in March

    In 2023, riding the first wave of GenAI excitement, Meridian funded Project Athena: a field-force copilot, launched org-wide in a single release, announced on stage at the national sales meet — in the same quarter as two major submission deadlines.     Athena worked in the demo and failed in the field: it hadn't met the field force's real data (Hinglish voice notes, offline entries, three CRM variants — a Data DNA mismatch nobody had tested). Reps abandoned it in six weeks and built spreadsheet workarounds. Direct write-off: ₹1.8 crore. But the real ledger was the aftermath. Confidence: for two years, every AI proposal at Meridian met the same three words — "remember Project Athena." Two of the four data scientists left. Funding: the CFO quietly added a skepticism premium — AI proposals now needed twice the evidence for half the budget, and the AI line vanished from one annual plan entirely. Value chain: worst of all, the field-reporting chain ended up worse than baseline — the workaround spreadsheets fragmented data that had been merely messy before, and the reps who'd been burned distrusted the next tool on principle.

    The write-off was ₹1.8 crore. The two-year freeze, the attrition, the skepticism premium and the degraded chain were the real cost — a multiple of it, paid by every initiative that came after.

    Portfolio shape — and the absorption constraint

    The selection shape: two quick compounders + one strategic build + zero vanity pilots. Then two decisions the shape rule alone misses:

    Stack or spread? Stacking two funded use cases on one value chain (extraction + QC triage, both attacking the AE chain) makes a chain metric move visibly — and in year one, proof beats breadth. Spreading diversifies risk but risks three task-wins no CFO can find. The rule: early portfolios stack at least two use cases on one chain, deliberately.

    Can BAU absorb it? Chapter 4 scores change load per use case — but change load aggregates. Three use cases each scoring a comfortable 4 can land on the same team in the same quarter: individually fine, collectively disruptive. A portfolio that starts with discovery and no delivery teaches the organisation that AI means experiments.

    Waves, not big-bang

    The portfolio is incremental and iterative by design: 90-day waves of 2–3 use cases, each running one loop — and the re-score step is where iteration becomes real, because wave 1's actual change load, actual drift and actual chain movement reprice wave 2's scores.     A use case "delivered" but not absorbed into BAU moves no chain metric — it's a demo with a budget. Integration into BAU is the value event, because the chain metrics live in BAU. Which is why the portfolio needs an owner for the thing no framework fully captures: running the business and changing the business at the same time. Managing BAU and the portfolio together is an art — the frameworks in this module carry the mechanics; a named role carries the judgment.

    The Portfolio Leader — the role this chapter creates

    Owns: the wave cadence and re-scoring · the team-level absorption view · the change calendar and rollback authority (BAU's protector) · chain-metric reporting to leadership · the entry gate. Reports: into the COO / transformation office — not into IT, because the asset being managed is the organisation's capacity to change, not the technology. Temperament: patience and calculated moves — the anti-Athena. This role seeds Pillar 2's leadership agenda.

    The Workshop Playbook

    Everything before this was the instrument. This chapter is the performance: how to run the prioritization workshop inside your own organisation — because the real assessment of this module isn't the quiz, it's this, within 30 days.

    A workshop is a seven-week arc, not a meeting

    Fig 8.1 — The arc. Three weeks of preparation, 120 minutes of decision, and the four weeks that decide whether anything was real. Case Study A shows this arc executed at Meridian.

    Before the room

    The sponsor contract — agreed at T − 3 weeks, in writing

    The workshop fails in advance or not at all. Before anything else, the sponsor (COO-level or equivalent) explicitly agrees to four things: (1) the scores decide — including the sponsor's own candidates, with no side door; (2) kills are a success outcome and will be announced as decisions, not buried; (3) the pre-conditions register gets funded with the portfolio, not "reviewed later"; (4) a Portfolio-Leader-designate will be named in the room. A sponsor who hesitates on any of the four isn't ready — postpone the workshop, not the standards.

    Casting the room — 8 to 12, never more

    This is a decision meeting with working sessions, not a town hall. The cast: the decision-makers who own budgets, the process owners whose chains are on the table, the data owner who can answer gate questions live, and a finance partner who will later own the bridge. Meridian's cast of nine: COO (sponsor) · Head of PV Operations · RA lead · QA head · Head of Field Excellence · IT/data platform lead · FP&A partner · Digital lead (facilitator) · PMO lead (scribe, and — as it turned out — Portfolio-Leader-designate). No vendor in the room. Ever.

    The pre-work packet and the mining pull

    Out at T − 1 week: Reader Ch 1–2 (~25 min), five candidate ideas per participant, and the 48-hour nudge (Design Document §2 flagged this as load-bearing — an empty-handed cohort halves the session's value). Meanwhile the facilitator pulls 90 days of timestamps from one core system and prepares a Fig-3.2-style mined map. It will be the workshop's most-argued-about exhibit — which is the point.

    In the room: mechanics that survive hierarchy

    Three roles: facilitator (owns the clock and the evidence question), scribe (owns the rationale lines — the words that survive board challenge), and the Portfolio-Leader-designate (owns the absorption view forming in real time; Chapter 7's role is born in this room). Three ground rules on the wall: score with evidence · kill is success · no vetoes before scoring.

    The six hard questions — and the answers that hold

    The question (verbatim from real rooms)The answer that holds
    "Our data is a mess. Why score anything before fixing it?"Ch 5's reframe: readiness is use-case-relative. Score the chains, not the enterprise — some are ready today, and the audit turns the rest into priced work orders. "Fix the data first" is a two-year detour wearing prudence as a costume.
    "Why not weight the dimensions? Surely impact matters more."Rank first (Ch 4). Weighting debates consume the hour and rarely change the top three. If a real decision later hinges on a tie — data readiness breaks it, always.
    "The CEO already promised X to the board."Then X goes through the gate first, publicly. If it scores well, the promise gains a defence. If it doesn't, better the room finds out than the market — and the sponsor signed clause 1 for exactly this moment.
    "Can't each business unit just pick its own use cases?"That's how the idea glut formed. One gate, one portfolio, absorption managed across teams (Ch 7) — BUs source candidates through the four lenses; they don't get private scoring rules.
    "The vendor says 96% accuracy. Isn't that settled?"Run the Ch 6 vendor trap live: accurate on which class? Show the confusion matrix on our critical class, the FN rate at the recommended threshold, and the Data DNA match — then offer the gold-standard set. Rooms enjoy this one; let them.
    "This feels slow. Our competitor is moving faster."Fig 7.2, and if the cohort read Case B, one word: Athena. The competitor's press release is not your deadline — it's how adrenaline decisions get made. Waves are the fast path measured over a year rather than a quarter.
